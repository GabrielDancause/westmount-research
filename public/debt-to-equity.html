Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good debt-to-equity ratio?

A "good" debt-to-equity (D/E) ratio depends heavily on the industry. Generally, a ratio below 1.0 is considered conservative, meaning the company relies more on equity than debt for financing. Ratios between 1.0 and 2.0 are typical for many established companies. Capital-intensive industries like utilities or telecom often safely maintain ratios above 2.0 because of their stable, predictable cash flows.

Which companies have the most debt?

In absolute terms, large banks, telecommunications giants (like AT&T and Verizon), and major utility companies hold the largest amounts of nominal debt. However, when looking at the ratio of debt compared to equity, companies that have bought back massive amounts of stock or suffered significant losses often appear the most heavily leveraged on paper.

Is high debt-to-equity always bad?

Not always. High leverage can amplify returns for shareholders when a company earns more on its borrowed money than the interest it pays. This is common in real estate and utilities. However, elevated debt becomes dangerous during economic downturns or when interest rates rise, as higher servicing costs can quickly erode profits and threaten solvency.

What sector has the highest leverage?

Utilities, Real Estate (REITs), and Telecommunications typically operate with the highest debt-to-equity ratios. These sectors require massive upfront capital expenditures but benefit from long-term, predictable revenue streams that allow them to comfortably service higher debt loads.

How does debt-to-equity affect stock price?

Companies with low D/E ratios are often viewed as safer investments, which can lead to more stable stock prices during market volatility. Conversely, highly leveraged companies can experience greater stock price volatility. If interest rates rise, the stock prices of highly leveraged firms often face downward pressure due to anticipated increases in debt servicing costs.

Methodology

Data was collected directly from public financial filings and parsed via Yahoo Finance APIs for S&P 500 components. "Total Debt" includes both short-term and long-term liabilities. The Debt-to-Equity ratio is calculated as Total Debt divided by Total Shareholders' Equity. Companies exhibiting negative equity (liabilities exceed assets) have their D/E ratio flagged, as standard calculations lose meaning in these scenarios. "Net Debt" subtracts cash and cash equivalents from Total Debt.
